\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Robots and wearable augmented-reality systems must use a map while the
environment is still being observed. A visually convincing map is needed for
view synthesis, while accurate geometry is needed for interaction, occlusion,
and spatial reasoning. Final reconstruction quality is therefore not the only
criterion: a region that becomes accurate only after the camera has moved on
is of limited use to an online agent. This motivates our central question:
\emph{how quickly can an online mapper make the appearance and geometry of a
newly observed region reliable?}

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) is a compelling representation for this problem.
Its explicit primitives and differentiable rasterizer support high-quality
rendering and incremental optimization~\cite{kerbl20233dgs}, and have enabled
several photorealistic SLAM systems~\cite{matsuki2024monogs,splatam2024,
huang2024photoslam,rtgslam2024}. However, rendering speed, input-processing
rate, and map convergence describe different properties. A system may render
an inaccurate map at high frame rate or keep pace with a camera stream while
leaving recently observed areas under-optimized. Real-time operation is thus a
necessary system constraint, but it does not by itself establish that a useful
map is formed quickly.

We focus on \emph{fast photometric and geometric convergence}. Because an
online map grows as new observations arrive, convergence here does not mean
asymptotic convergence to a single fixed-scene optimum. We use the term
operationally: after a region is first observed, photometric convergence is the
rate at which its held-out rendering error decreases, and geometric convergence
is the rate at which its surface and free-space errors decrease. Both are
measured against elapsed time and completed mapping updates. This view exposes
a distinction that final-only evaluation hides: two systems can reach similar
endpoint quality while making that quality available at very different times.

Fast photometric convergence first requires the mapper to use observations for
their mapping value rather than inherit the tracking policy unchanged. In a
DROID-SLAM-style frontend, keyframes are selected to maintain pose and dense
correspondence estimation~\cite{teed2021droid}. They are indispensable for
tracking, but they are not the only images that constrain scene appearance.
Frames between keyframes supply additional viewpoints and texture evidence.
Adding all such frames immediately, however, enlarges the optimization set
faster than a fixed mapping budget can refine it; uniform sampling from the
growing set can also leave newly available temporal regions with substantially
fewer updates. The relevant problem is therefore not simply to use more
frames, but to control when they enter mapping and how the available updates
are distributed across them.

Fast photometric improvement does not guarantee fast geometric improvement.
Alpha compositing allows color, opacity, scale, and position to compensate for
one another, so photometric loss can be small even when opacity lies at an
incorrect depth~\cite{deng2022dsnerf,stablegs}. Depth and normal supervision
reduce this ambiguity~\cite{huang20242dgs,chen2024pgsr}, but a loss on a single
rendered depth statistic need not identify separated opacity modes along the
ray. In particular, Gaussian opacity in front of an observed surface can
survive when color gradients become weak. An online method therefore needs a
geometric signal that acts directly on such free-space violations without
requiring expensive global reconstruction or post-processing.

We address the two convergence bottlenecks within a DROID-SLAM-based Gaussian
SLAM architecture. To accelerate photometric convergence, we decouple the
mapping-view set from the tracking keyframe set. Additional, causally available
frames are incorporated at a rate paced by completed mapping updates, and our
Entropy-Regularized Count Balancing (ERCB) sampler applies a bounded correction
to temporal groups that have received substantially fewer updates than the
current average. Sampling without replacement is retained to maintain view
diversity. To accelerate geometric convergence, we currently instantiate the
framework with a one-sided Carve loss: depth-supported free-space evidence
reduces the opacity of Gaussians that lie in front of observed surfaces, while
leaving unobserved space unconstrained. The two components share one objective:
to make each unit of online optimization produce useful appearance and geometry
earlier in the trajectory.

We evaluate this objective directly rather than infer it from a final map.
Controlled incremental experiments hold poses, depths, initialization, input
order, and optimization budgets fixed to isolate mapping behavior. We report
held-out photometric and geometric quality as functions of both wall-clock time
and completed updates, including free-space violations and completeness so that
removing geometry cannot be mistaken for reconstructing it. End-to-end
experiments then test whether the same gains survive competition with the
tracking frontend at the native input rate.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate online 3DGS mapping around \emph{fast photometric and
    geometric convergence}, separating the rate at which map quality improves
    from rendering speed and stream-processing throughput.
    \item We introduce a mapping-view policy that expands supervision beyond
    tracking keyframes according to completed mapping work, together with ERCB,
    which corrects severe update-count deficits while preserving randomized
    sampling without replacement.
    \item Under the current Carve instantiation, we introduce opacity-directed
    free-space supervision from causal depth evidence to suppress premature ray
    termination without forcing all Gaussians onto the first visible surface.
    \item We establish an evaluation protocol based on appearance and geometry
    convergence curves under matched update and wall-clock budgets, followed by
    a separate end-to-end online test.
\end{itemize}
